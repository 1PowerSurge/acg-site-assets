Best Custom T-Shirt Printing Options for Small Quantities

March 18, 2025

Custom t-shirts for a small event, a local business promotion, or a client's team do not require a large minimum order to look professional — but the method you choose matters more at low quantities than it does when you are ordering by the hundreds. Three main methods cover most small-run apparel work: direct-to-garment printing, screen printing, and heat transfer. Each has a real use case, and each has situations where it falls apart.

Direct-to-Garment: The Best Starting Point for Small Orders

Direct-to-garment printing, or DTG, works by essentially inkjet-printing your design directly onto the fabric. It handles full-color artwork with gradients and photographic detail without any additional setup cost. For a quantity of 1 to around 24 shirts, DTG is almost always the most cost-effective option because there are no screens to burn and no setup fees — each shirt is essentially printed on demand.

The main constraint with DTG is fabric. It performs best on 100% ring-spun cotton. Polyester blends reduce ink absorption and can result in faded or washed-out color, particularly in darker ink areas. If your shirts are a 50/50 cotton-poly blend, discuss this with your printer before placing the order. Pre-treatment of the garment is also important for DTG on dark shirts — this is typically handled by the printer, but confirm it is included.

Screen Printing: Right for Repeated Designs at 12 or More

Screen printing is the traditional method and still produces the most vibrant, durable result for solid-color designs. Each color in the design requires its own screen, and setting those screens up takes time and money. That setup cost makes screen printing impractical for 1-5 shirts, but once you reach 12 or more pieces with the same design, the per-shirt cost drops significantly and the quality is hard to beat.

Screen printing works on a wide variety of fabrics, including blends, and the ink sits on top of the fabric in a way that holds up through many washes. If you are doing a small local event with 20 or more identical shirts, screen printing is worth the conversation with your printer. Just know that changing colors between sizes or adding individual names to each shirt quickly complicates the cost structure.

Heat Transfer: Useful but Know the Limitations

Heat transfer involves printing a design onto a special transfer paper or film, then pressing it onto the garment with a heat press. This method is fast, requires minimal setup, and can handle individual custom names or numbers efficiently — making it practical for sports teams or personalized gifts. However, heat transfer prints can crack or peel over time, especially if the application temperature was not correct or if the garment is washed frequently at high heat.

There are quality differences in heat transfer materials. Consumer-grade transfers from an inkjet printer at home are not the same as commercial plastisol or digital heat transfers from a print shop. If durability matters to your client, clarify what type of transfer is being used before committing.

File Requirements and Placement

For all three methods, start with the cleanest file you can supply. PNG with a transparent background at 300dpi at the intended print size is a reliable format for DTG and heat transfer. For screen printing, vector art (AI or EPS) is preferred, especially for designs with clean edges and spot colors. Standard placements are center chest (roughly 3-4 inches below the collar), left chest (a smaller logo, pocket-area position), and full back. Confirm exact placement dimensions with your printer, as garment sizes affect where center chest actually falls.
